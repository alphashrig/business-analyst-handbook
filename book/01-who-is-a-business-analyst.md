## The one-sentence definition

> A **Business Analyst** helps an organisation understand **what problem it really has**, **what a good solution must do**, and **whether the delivered solution actually solved it**.

The BA is the **bridge** between the business (the people with the problem and the money) and the delivery team (the people who build the solution).

```
   BUSINESS SIDE                         BUSINESS ANALYST                     DELIVERY SIDE
 ─────────────────                     ───────────────────                 ─────────────────
  Client / sponsor      needs, goals     Understands, questions,   clear,     Developers
  End users           ───────────────▶   analyses, documents,   ──────────▶   Testers
  Operations           ◀───────────────  validates               ◀──────────   Architects
  Compliance            demos, impact    and traces requirements  questions,  UX designers
                                                                  estimates
```

## What a BA is *not*

New BAs are often pulled into work that isn't theirs. Knowing the boundaries protects you and the project.

| A BA is **not**... | Because... |
|--------------------|------------|
| A note-taker or "requirements secretary" | You analyse and challenge, not just write down |
| The Project Manager | The PM owns time, cost, resources and delivery risk ([see the comparison](../roles/ba-vs-project-manager.md)) |
| The person who decides priorities alone | The business (sponsor or Product Owner) decides; you advise |
| A developer or architect | You define *what* is needed; they design *how* to build it |
| The tester | QA verifies the build; you make sure what is tested is the right thing |

## The core responsibilities, in plain words

1. **Understand the business.** How it works today, who is involved, where it hurts.
2. **Find the real problem.** Clients often ask for a *solution* ("we need an app"). Your job is to find the *need* behind it ("customers wait 40 minutes on hold to report a claim").
3. **Elicit requirements.** Draw out what stakeholders need through interviews, workshops, observation and document review.
4. **Analyse and model.** Turn conversations into process maps, data models, rules and use cases. Spot gaps and conflicts.
5. **Document and communicate.** Write requirements that are clear, testable and agreed.
6. **Manage requirements over time.** Track changes, keep traceability, keep everyone aligned.
7. **Validate the solution.** Support testing and UAT to confirm the solution meets the need.
8. **Measure value.** After go-live, check whether the promised benefits arrived.

## Why the BA matters in an ICT company

Industry studies have repeatedly found that **poor requirements are one of the top causes of IT project failure**. Fixing a misunderstood requirement after go-live costs many times more than fixing it during analysis. That gap is what a good BA closes.

In an ICT services company specifically, the BA also protects the **commercial relationship**:

- Clear scope means fewer disputes about "that was included" vs "that's a change request".
- Well-understood needs mean more accurate estimates and fixed-price bids.
- Satisfied clients come back with the next project.

## Key qualities of a good BA

| Quality | What it looks like in practice |
|---------|-------------------------------|
| Curiosity | Asking "why?" until you reach the real need |
| Structured thinking | Breaking a messy problem into clear parts |
| Communication | Explaining tech to business and business to tech |
| Neutrality | Representing all stakeholders fairly, not just the loudest |
| Attention to detail | Catching the edge case nobody mentioned |
| Facilitation | Running a workshop so everyone contributes and decisions get made |
| Courage | Saying "this requirement conflicts with that one" in a room full of senior people |
